STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

Lexical Hazards

* “det kristelige” does not merely mean “what is Christian” in a generic descriptive sense; it denotes what accords with the nature and principle of Christian life.
* “frihet” is the governing theological and ecclesiological term. It means freedom, but not autonomy or absence of obligation. It is freedom arising from the Spirit and from inward willingness.
* “tvang” means compulsion or coercion. The contrast is not between freedom and discipline, but between Spirit-wrought willingness and externally imposed constraint.
* “ydelser og tjenester” refers to contributions and services rendered in the church. “Contributions and service” preserves the institutional as well as moral sense.
* “den indre tvang” is paradoxical: the church rejects outward coercion so that giving and service may be “compelled” from within. The phrase denotes inward spiritual constraint, not external force.
* “kjødet” is the Pauline “flesh,” not merely the physical body. It denotes fallen human nature resistant to the Spirit.
* “undertvinges” means subdued, brought under, subjected. It retains strong ascetical and Pauline force.
* “Faar Aanden raade” means “if the Spirit is allowed to rule” or “if the Spirit has rule.” “Rule” is preferable because of the moral government implied.
* “kræve” means demand or claim, particularly in contrast to giving.
* “præsteskole” is literally “pastors’ school” or “school for pastors,” but in this institutional context “seminary” would be too interpretive. “School for pastors” or “ministerial school” preserves the historical designation.
* “den frie menighet” refers to the free congregation/church, with an ecclesiological significance specific to the Lutheran Free Church setting.
* “bestaaen” means continued existence, maintenance, or survival.
* “orker at gjøre” is stronger than merely “wants to do”: it asks what one has strength or capacity to do.
* “trængselstider” carries biblical resonance: times of distress, tribulation, or severe hardship.
* “nybygge” refers to newly settled frontier districts or settlements.
* “formaaede” means were able, had strength/capacity.
* “frihetstrang” is difficult to reproduce compactly. It means a longing, impulse, or drive toward freedom.
* “aand” in “mangel paa frihetstrang og aand” means spirit in the moral-spiritual sense, not merely enthusiasm.
* “subskriptioner” means pledged subscriptions or promised contributions.
* “vil vi noget?” is rhetorically sharper than “do we want anything?” It asks whether there is any effective will or resolve among them.
* “fattigdom paa aand” means poverty of spirit in the sense of spiritual poverty or lack of spiritual vigor, not the positive beatitude “poor in spirit.”
* “gjort indsamlingen fra sig” means completed or disposed of their assigned collection.
* “avlingen slog feil” means the crop failed.
* “opgjøre vor avgjorte mening” means settle or make firm our decided determination.
* “kraftanstrengelse” is a concentrated exertion of strength, a vigorous effort.
* “tro paa et heldig udfald” means faith/confidence in a successful outcome.
* “død kirkelighet” is “dead churchliness” or “dead ecclesiasticism”: outward church form without spiritual life.

Syntactic Hazards

* The opening argument proceeds through several linked antitheses: freedom versus compulsion, outward coercion versus inward constraint, flesh versus Spirit, demanding versus giving. Breaking these into short modern sentences would weaken the cumulative logic.
* Several sentences employ parataxis and semicolon-like rhetorical pivots even when punctuation is simple. English should preserve the accumulating sermon cadence.
* “Men friheten er dyrekjøpt og bevares ikke uten kamp, kamp imot kjødet…” deliberately repeats “kamp” (“struggle”) to intensify the moral argument.
* “Faar Aanden raade blir vi i frihet, som vi blir i Herrens ord” is compressed and syntactically unusual. The translation must preserve its association of the Spirit’s rule, freedom, and abiding in the Lord’s word.
* The Augsburg paragraphs build historically by comparison: past poverty and sacrifice, present prosperity, present moral test.
* The question “vil vi noget?” is intentionally abrupt after a longer historical argument and should remain abrupt.
* The discussion of the current collection moves through concession, qualification, hope, and fear in a single rhetorical progression.
* The final paragraph returns from institutional fundraising to spiritual combat. This widening of scope must remain evident.

Theological Hazards

* Freedom is explicitly grounded in the Holy Spirit, with 2 Corinthians 3:17 as the controlling biblical statement.
* “Flesh” must retain its Pauline theological meaning as that which opposes Spirit-created freedom.
* Christian giving is represented not as voluntary philanthropy in a modern secular sense but as the fruit of inward spiritual freedom.
* “Spirit” in the article can refer both to the Holy Spirit and, contextually, to spiritual vigor or disposition. These senses must not be collapsed.
* The survival of Augsburg is interpreted spiritually: material sacrifice becomes evidence of inward freedom.
* Failure to act despite prosperity is treated as spiritual testimony against the church, not merely administrative failure.
* The final warning contrasts church life “created by the Spirit of God” with worldliness and dead churchliness. The life/death polarity is theological.
* “Tro og du skal se Guds herlighet” invokes the biblical connection between faith and beholding God’s glory, likely John 11:40.

Voice Hazards

* The author’s tone is admonitory but not bombastic. Moral pressure emerges from comparison between earlier sacrifice and present ability.
* The repeated “we” implicates writer and readers together. It must not become detached institutional commentary.
* Historical financial figures are used rhetorically as evidence in a spiritual indictment.
* The article deliberately refuses sentimental fundraising language. Its appeal is to Christian freedom, resolve, sacrifice, and spiritual life.
* The closing petition intensifies sharply into a call for struggle against worldliness and spiritual superficiality. This should not be softened into generalized encouragement.
* Phrases such as “a much too sad testimony” and “poverty of spirit” must retain their stern evaluative force.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

* “frihet eller tvang” is more than an administrative opposition. It establishes the spiritual antithesis governing the entire article: willing action created by the Spirit versus action extracted by external pressure.
* “fremtvinges av den indre tvang” is intentionally paradoxical. A literal English rendering such as “forced forth by inward compulsion” preserves the paradox better than “motivated internally.”
* “kamp imot kjødet” is Pauline language. Compare Romans 7–8, Galatians 5:16–24, and related flesh/Spirit antitheses. “Struggle against the flesh” preserves the biblical category.
* “Hvor Herrens aand er, der er frihet” quotes or closely follows 2 Corinthians 3:17. The familiar English form is “Where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty/freedom.” Because the translation profile resists inherited KJV diction, “freedom” is preferable to “liberty.”
* “Faar Aanden raade” is pietistic and devotional language for permitting the Holy Spirit to govern the life.
* “ikke bare kræve, men først og fremst gi” establishes the ethical consequence of Spirit-given freedom: the Christian does not primarily insist upon claims but gives.
* “frie mænd og kvinder” should not be modernized into “independent people.” It means persons inwardly free for willing sacrifice.
* “Friheten spørger ikke efter hvad andre gjør” personifies freedom. The metaphor should remain: freedom itself “does not ask what others are doing.”
* “hvad den selv orker at gjøre” expresses capacity joined to willing exertion. “What it itself has strength to do” is more faithful than “what it can afford.”
* “trængselstider” carries scriptural overtones of affliction and tribulation. “Times of distress” keeps the register without importing archaic KJV diction.
* “redde Augsburg fra undergang” literally means save Augsburg from ruin/destruction. The strength should be retained.
* “et meget sterkt vidnesbyrd” means strong testimony or witness. This reflects a theological habit of treating visible action as evidence of inward spiritual reality.
* “vil vi noget?” is idiomatic and forceful: “do we have any will?” would be too literal; “do we really will anything?” preserves its moral edge.
* “fattigdom paa aand” should not be translated “poverty in spirit,” because that can evoke Matthew 5:3 positively. “Spiritual poverty” is clearer here.
* “den bedste tid av aaret … er jo gaat” uses “jo” to mark something presumed or obvious: “the best time of the year … has, after all, passed.”
* “opgjøre vor avgjorte mening” means to come to a settled determination. Literal “make up our decided opinion” would be awkward and obscure.
* “Ikke ord, men handling vil alene gjøre det” is a compact maxim: “Not words, but action alone will do it.”
* “kjæmpe os frem til tro” is a striking devotional expression: to struggle one’s way toward faith/confidence. It should not be smoothed into “remain optimistic.”
* “Tro og du skal se Guds herlighet” echoes biblical diction and should retain imperative force: “Believe, and you shall see the glory of God.”
* “kamp i alle troende mennesker” means an inward struggle arising in believers, not conflict among believers.
* “kvæles av verdslighet” preserves a physical metaphor: church life may be “choked” or “stifled” by worldliness.
* “død kirkelighet” denotes lifeless ecclesiastical religiosity. “Dead churchliness” is unusual English but accurately retains the author’s polemical concreteness.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

* Thesis: Christian church life is properly governed by freedom rather than outward compulsion.
* Qualification: This freedom is costly and must continually be defended through struggle against the flesh.
* Doctrinal foundation: Where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom; when the Spirit rules, Christians become free and move from demanding to giving.
* Institutional application: The current fundraising effort for Augsburg raises the question whether this freedom is truly present.
* Historical claim: Augsburg has remained a free institution only because free men, women, and congregations willingly sacrificed for it.
* Definition of genuine freedom: Freedom does not wait to see what others will do; it asks what it itself has strength to do.
* Historical evidence: During the poverty and hardship of the 1870s, impoverished settlers nevertheless eliminated a $16,000 debt and saved Augsburg.
* Present comparison: In 1917 the debt is $17,000, while the people are substantially more prosperous.
* Moral inference: Failure now would therefore testify not to lack of financial power but to lack of spiritual freedom and spirit.
* Supporting historical evidence: Poor pastors and laypeople formerly pledged amounts that were large relative to their means.
* Rhetorical pivot: Financial strength is now greater; the real question is whether the will remains.
* Strong judgment: It ought to be inconceivable that Augsburg should remain in debt this year; such failure would be evidence of spiritual poverty.
* Qualified assessment of the current collection: The completed collections are encouraging, but fewer than one third of congregations had completed them by New Year.
* Mixed outlook: If remaining congregations act similarly, hope remains; yet the best fundraising season has passed, and crop failure limits some western congregations.
* Practical appeal: The church must settle firmly that the debt will be raised this year so that the next fiscal year begins debt-free.
* Rejection of mere rhetoric: Further writing is secondary; action alone can accomplish the goal.
* Corroborating testimony: An older pastor calls for a concentrated effort and expresses a struggle toward faith in a successful outcome.
* Biblical confirmation: “Believe, and you shall see the glory of God.”
* Final enlargement: The financial campaign is finally presented as one manifestation of a broader spiritual struggle against worldliness, superficiality, and dead churchliness.
* Concluding force: The survival of Spirit-created congregational life itself is at stake.

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

* “Hvor Herrens aand er, der er frihet.” Direct quotation or very close citation of 2 Corinthians 3:17: “where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom.”
* “kamp imot kjødet” echoes the Pauline conflict between flesh and Spirit, especially Galatians 5:16–17 and Romans 8:5–13.
* “Faar Aanden raade” belongs to the same Pauline pattern of life governed by the Spirit.
* The movement from demanding to giving corresponds broadly to New Testament teaching on freely given grace producing willing generosity, especially 2 Corinthians 8–9.
* “trængselstider” evokes biblical language of affliction or tribulation, though no single verse need be intended.
* “vidnesbyrd” functions with biblical resonance as visible testimony to an inward spiritual condition.
* “Tro og du skal se Guds herlighet.” This is an adaptation of John 11:40, where Jesus says that if one believes, one will see the glory of God.
* “menighetslivet, skapt av Guds aand” reflects New Testament teaching concerning the church as constituted and enlivened by the Holy Spirit.
* The opposition between Spirit-created life and “dead churchliness” recalls broader biblical contrasts of Spirit and letter, life and death, including 2 Corinthians 3:6.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

* The opening must retain certainty: the author says there is no need even to ask whether Christianity belongs with freedom or compulsion.
* “The flesh” must remain an enemy of freedom and something that “must always be subdued.”
* The comparison between the 1870s and 1917 must retain its accusatory implication: materially poorer predecessors accomplished what materially stronger successors may fail to accomplish.
* “vil vi noget?” must retain its abrupt challenge to the reader’s will.
* “spiritual poverty” must remain a grave judgment, not be softened into “lack of enthusiasm.”
* The closing call for struggle must sound necessary rather than inspirational.
* Worldliness and dead churchliness must remain forces capable of choking the life created by the Spirit of God.

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

* “maa undertvinges” → “must always be subdued,” preserving necessity.
* “vil vi ikke bare kræve” → “we will not merely demand,” preserving consequence rather than weak preference.
* “Dersom vi nu ikke har kraft…” → retains the conditional accusation without mitigation.
* “skal bli gjældfri” → “shall become debt-free” / “must become debt-free.” In context, the author presents this as an imperative expectation; “must” best carries the practical force in English.
* “skal samles, og samles iaar” → “must be raised, and raised this year,” preserving repeated necessity.
* “kan vel ikke gjøre” → “can hardly do,” retaining practical limitation.
* “vil alene gjøre det” → “action alone will do it,” retaining exclusivity.
* “Maatte…” → optative prayer/wish: “May…” rather than a weakened “I hope that…”

STEP 8: METAPHOR INTEGRITY PRESERVATION

* Freedom remains personified as something that “asks” what it itself has strength to do.
* Freedom “carried Augsburg through” its times of distress; this supporting/bearing metaphor should remain.
* The flesh remains an “enemy of freedom.”
* “struggle” must recur throughout the piece, creating continuity between personal sanctification, institutional sacrifice, faith, and the church’s resistance to worldliness.
* Church life remains something capable of being “choked” by worldliness and dead churchliness.
* The language of spiritual poverty remains intact as a moral-spiritual metaphor.

STEP 9: TONE CONTRAST PRESERVATION

* The article begins doctrinally and confidently.
* It then moves into historical gratitude for sacrifice.
* That gratitude turns into rebuke when the prosperous present is compared with the impoverished past.
* The author briefly allows encouragement when discussing collections already completed.
* Hope is immediately balanced with fear because of delay and crop failure.
* The practical appeal then becomes terse and resolute: words are insufficient.
* The quoted pastor introduces tentative faith and renewed encouragement.
* The final paragraph rises again into severe spiritual warning and prayerful exhortation.
* These fluctuations should remain distinct rather than being harmonized into a uniformly uplifting fundraising appeal.

STEP 10: ORAL CADENCE AWARENESS

* The opening should retain its repeated key nouns—freedom, compulsion, struggle, flesh, Spirit—because they function almost as oral refrain.
* Long sentences should be preserved where the argument accumulates through cause and consequence.
* Abrupt interrogatives such as “But we must ask: do we will anything?” should interrupt the longer cadence.
* Repetitions such as “struggle … struggle” and “must be raised, and raised this year” carry oral emphasis and should remain.
* The final sentence should remain extended, since its accumulating enemies—worldliness, spiritual superficiality, dead churchliness—produce the concluding pressure.

STEP 11: ACADEMIC TRANSLATION

FREEDOM OR COMPULSION

(E. E. Gynild.)

——

We need not ask which is Christian, freedom or compulsion. We have therefore sought to live in freedom in our church life, so that both contributions and services should be brought forth by inward compulsion. But freedom is dearly bought and is not preserved without struggle, struggle against the flesh, which must always be subdued, because it is an enemy of freedom. “Where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom.” If the Spirit is allowed to rule, we come into freedom, as we do in the word of the Lord. Then we will not merely demand, but first and foremost give.

When I have thought about the collection for our school for pastors, this question of freedom has pressed itself upon me. Augsburg is a free institution, placed in the service of the free congregation. It has lived and preserved its freedom through many struggles and much poverty, because there were free men and women, and some free congregations, who willingly sacrificed and labored for its continued existence.

Freedom does not ask what others are doing. It asks only what it itself has strength to do. And in labor of that kind there is both inspiration and encouragement. It is freedom of this kind that has carried Augsburg through the times of distress through which it has passed.

In the seventies the school had a debt of $16,000.00. The grasshoppers had ravaged the West, and our people in the new settlements were poor; nevertheless they were able to raise the money for the debt and save Augsburg from ruin. In 1917 we had a debt of $17,000.00, and now our people are for the most part prosperous. If we now do not have the strength to make the school debt-free, then it is a very strong testimony to our lack of longing for freedom and of spirit.

I shall also remind you that in several collections since the Lutheran Free Church was organized around Augsburg, poor pastors gave subscriptions ranging from $25 up to $100, and among a portion of the congregational people the proportion was the same. We willed it, and we carried out what we willed. Our financial strength is greater now, but we must ask: do we will anything?

It is impossible to conceive that we should not make Augsburg debt-free this year. It would be far too sad a testimony to spiritual poverty.

We shall not complain about the collection this year where it has been carried out. But not one third of the congregations had completed their collection or sent in their contributions by New Year. Up to January 4, only 100 congregations had finished their collection. When we take into consideration the amount for which receipts had been issued, that was indeed rather encouraging. If those who remain would now do something similar, there would indeed be reason for hope. But the best time of the year for a collection has, after all, passed, and congregations in that region of the West where the crop failed can hardly do what they would have done in a good year; thus the outlook may give occasion both for fear and for hope.

This is written in order that we may settle it as our firm determination that the debt must be raised, and raised this year, so that the next fiscal year may begin without debt. I shall not weary you with much writing hereafter. Not words, but action alone will do it.

One of the older pastors writes in a letter of January 16:

“May the friends now in their great majority see it in this way, as he sees it, and there might come something of a mighty exertion in the work, and without doubt it would succeed.”

“We are trying to struggle our way forward to faith in a successful outcome.”

“Believe, and you shall see the glory of God.”

Thus far the letter.

May there arise a struggle in all believing people throughout the congregations, a struggle against worldliness and all spiritual superficiality, so that congregational life, created by the Spirit of God, may not be choked by worldliness and dead churchliness.
