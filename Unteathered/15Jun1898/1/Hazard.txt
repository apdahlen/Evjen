STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

Lexical Hazards

* “Frikirke” means “Free Church,” not merely an independent congregation. It denotes a particular ecclesiological principle: freedom of the congregation from coercive church structures and, in this context, the emerging Lutheran Free Church.
* “Kald” in “Past. Gjertsens Kald” is ecclesiastical. It refers to a pastor’s charge or parish rather than simply his personal “calling.”
* “Herrens Gjerning” is “the Lord’s work,” with biblical and devotional force.
* “Menighed” may mean an individual congregation or the Church/congregation of believers according to context. The distinction matters greatly in the sermon.
* “Grundvold” is literally “foundation,” carrying the biblical architectural imagery of 1 Corinthians 3.
* “Hovedhjørnesten” is “chief cornerstone” or “cornerstone,” an explicitly biblical christological term.
* “retfærdiggjøres” is the doctrinal term “is justified,” and must not be weakened to “is made right” or “is accepted.”
* “levende Stene” is the biblical “living stones.”
* “levendegjorte” means “made alive” or “quickened,” with regeneration/spiritual-life implications.
* “helligede” means “sanctified,” not merely “made better” or “purified.”
* “usynlige Menighed” is the technical ecclesiological expression “invisible Church.”
* “Lemmer” means “members,” but evokes the Pauline image of members of Christ’s body.
* “Kjød og Blod” is the biblical idiom “flesh and blood,” not merely human opinion in a generic sense.
* “Vækkelse” is “awakening” or “revival,” with pietistic and Haugean associations.
* “det vakte Lægfolk” means “the awakened laity,” referring to spiritually awakened lay Christians.
* “høikirkelige” means “high-church” in ecclesiological orientation.
* “Levendegjørelse og Frigjørelse” combines spiritual quickening/revival with ecclesiastical liberation.
* “Samfund” means church body, denomination, or ecclesiastical association depending on context; translating it simply as “society” would mislead.
* “Samfundsforbindelse” means denominational affiliation.
* “personligt Kristenliv” means personal Christian life, with an emphasis on lived, individual faith.
* “Presteskole” means a school for the training of pastors; “theological seminary” is usually the clearest contextual rendering.
* “alvorlige, levende Herrens Vidner” means earnest, spiritually living witnesses of the Lord.
* “Missionssansen” means missionary concern, missionary consciousness, or missionary zeal.
* “Hedningemission” is the historical missionary category for mission among non-Christian peoples. A literal “heathen mission” preserves period terminology but sounds much harsher in contemporary English; “mission to the heathen” is historically transparent, while “foreign mission” would partially obscure the original category.
* “Indremission” is “home mission” or “inner mission,” the Lutheran-pietist work of evangelization and spiritual renewal among nominal Christians.
* “Opbyggelsesarbeidet” denotes work for Christian edification and spiritual awakening, not physical construction despite the literal component “building.”
* “Sjæles Omvendelse og Frelse” is “the conversion and salvation of souls.”
* “befindes tro” means “be found faithful,” with clear biblical resonance.
* “Railroad-Certifikaterne” refers to railroad certificates connected with convention travel arrangements or fare concessions; it should not be modernized into an invented administrative category.

Syntactic Hazards

* The opening sermon uses long cumulative sentences in which conditional clauses establish moral and theological necessity: “Men skal det ske, da maa …” The causal sequence must remain intact.
* The sermon repeatedly shifts from architectural illustration to ecclesiology without explicitly announcing every transition. The translation should retain this movement rather than explaining it.
* Parallel sequences such as “levende, ren, hellig Menighed” and “levendegjorte, rensede og helligede” are rhetorically deliberate.
* Rhetorical questions such as “Men hvor blev der af Herrens Menighed?” carry accusatory force and should not be converted into neutral observations.
* “Nei, vi har alle vor Skyld” is a sharp sermonic pivot. Its brevity must be retained.
* The minutes alternate abruptly between devotional prose, procedural resolutions, reports, quotations, and election records. These shifts belong to the document and should not be stylistically homogenized.
* Several paragraphs consist of compressed parliamentary language: “Besluttet at høre Indberetninger,” “Besluttet at skride til Valg.” English should remain concise and documentary.
* Historical punctuation and semicolon logic often carry more structural weight than modern English punctuation normally would.

Theological Hazards

* Christ as the only foundation must remain exclusive: “kun en Grundvold.”
* Justification “alone by faith in Christ” is a central Lutheran doctrinal formula and must remain exact.
* The sermon distinguishes the true spiritual nature of the Church from the imperfect empirical form of congregations on earth. This distinction must not be blurred.
* The visible/invisible Church issue is polemical. The preacher objects to using the doctrine of the invisible Church as an excuse for tolerating spiritual deadness in actual congregations.
* “living stones” denotes persons spiritually made alive by God’s Spirit, not merely active church members.
* “without spot or wrinkle” echoes Ephesians 5 and refers to ecclesial holiness.
* “light and salt,” “city on the hill,” and “body” are biblical ecclesiological images and should retain their familiar forms.
* The criticism of forming large congregations without examining “spiritual life and real Christianity” is central to the Free Church argument and should not be softened.
* “flesh and blood” versus “God’s good and perfect will” sets human preference against divine direction.
* The Haugean awakening is presented as an historical recovery of principles claimed to be “as old as Christianity itself.”
* “conscience” and “obedience” are crucial in the conflict between Free Church ideals and denominational authority.
* The seminary’s purpose is stated spiritually: to train “earnest, living witnesses of the Lord,” not merely professionally competent clergy.
* Institutional survival is subordinated to spiritual identity: the Free Church does not depend upon earthly property.
* Faithfulness “to the last” carries eschatological and biblical force.

Voice Hazards

* The sermon is admonitory rather than descriptive.
* Its criticism implicates the speaker together with his hearers: “we all bear our guilt.”
* The preacher does not attack only opponents; he subjects his own movement to judgment.
* The force comes from theological consequence rather than emotional embellishment.
* The institutional reports retain devotional language about blessing, fruit, faithfulness, and the Lord’s help; this should not be secularized into administrative language.
* Polemical passages concerning the Norwegian Synod and United Church are historically sharp and should remain so.
* The minutes themselves are restrained and factual, contrasting with the spiritual intensity of speeches and reports.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

* “lægge hinanden paa Hjerte” literally means to lay something upon one another’s heart. In English, “earnestly impress upon one another” or “lay upon one another’s hearts” preserves the devotional character. The latter retains the biblical-pietist idiom more fully.
* “se hen til” means to look to, regard, or direct oneself toward. In devotional usage it can carry the stronger sense of fixing one’s attention upon God’s will.
* “raadspørge Kjød og Blod” echoes the biblical expression “consult flesh and blood,” especially Galatians 1:16. A literal rendering is preferable because the biblical allusion is intelligible in English.
* “Staden paa Bjerget” is “the city on the hill,” echoing Matthew 5:14.
* “Lys og Salt i Verden” combines Matthew 5:13–14. “Light and salt in the world” should remain recognizable.
* “uden Plet eller Rynke” echoes Ephesians 5:27. “Without spot or wrinkle” is the natural biblical equivalent.
* “levende Lemmer paa hans Legeme” uses the Pauline body metaphor. “Living members of his body” preserves both theology and image.
* “Herrens Tempel” and “bygge paa Guds Tempel” are not figurative flourishes to be explained away; they structure the sermon’s ecclesiology.
* “levende Stene” is directly biblical. “Living stones” is the natural English equivalent.
* “Kjød og Blod” should remain “flesh and blood” rather than “human considerations.”
* “begynde i Tro” means more than optimistically beginning. It means commencing in reliance upon God: “begin in faith.”
* “trænge sig ind” in the discussion of Hauge’s principles means increasingly forcing themselves upon consciousness or gaining real entrance. “Begin in earnest to make themselves felt” preserves the historical argument.
* “Aand og Sind” means spirit and disposition/mind. In the polemical claim that Anti-Missourians remain “good Synod people,” it refers to their continuing ecclesiastical mentality.
* “arbeidet for Vækkelse og aandeligt Liv” means laboring for awakening and spiritual life rather than merely promoting religious enthusiasm.
* “under Trængsel og Modstand” carries the biblical-devotional sense of tribulation and opposition. “Amid tribulation and opposition” retains this gravity.
* “levende Prester og levende Menigheder” deliberately repeats “living.” This must not be varied into “vital pastors” and “flourishing congregations.”
* “befindes tro indtil det sidste” means “be found faithful to the last,” a biblical-sounding formula consistent with Revelation and New Testament perseverance language.
* “Missionssagen” literally means “the mission cause,” an older Protestant expression for missionary work and advocacy.
* “glædes ved Frugt af vort Arbeide” invokes the biblical image of spiritual fruit. “Rejoice in the fruit of our labor” is natural and preserves the metaphor.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

Opening sermon

* The meeting begins with the desire that the annual gathering become a blessing.
* The preacher immediately establishes a condition: this blessing is possible only if God himself directs them by his Spirit and Word.
* He introduces the controlling image from 1 Corinthians 3: the Lord’s temple and its building.
* First comes the foundation.
* He argues from ordinary construction: a bad foundation brings collapse and destruction.
* He transfers the analogy to the congregation of God.
* He identifies the only true foundation as Jesus Christ.
* He connects this christological foundation with Paul and then with Luther’s doctrine of justification by faith alone in Christ.
* He turns from foundation to workmanship: each person must consider how he builds.
* He turns from workmanship to building material.
* He distinguishes enduring material from inferior material through the apostolic imagery.
* He identifies the true material as “living stones”: people made alive, cleansed, and sanctified by God’s Spirit.
* He asserts that God desires a living, pure, holy Church.
* He rejects an appeal to the “invisible Church” when used to excuse the defects of empirical congregations.
* He defines the Lord’s congregation as those who through faith in Christ have become living members of his body.
* He then concedes the difficulty of manifesting this Church perfectly in earthly form.
* Judas and the disorders of apostolic congregations establish that visible imperfection is unavoidable.
* Yet this concession is immediately limited: imperfection is no justification for indiscriminately gathering nominal Christians into congregations.
* The preacher broadens responsibility rather than scapegoating particular men or church bodies.
* The confession “we all bear our guilt” forms the moral center of the rebuke.
* He contrasts impressive institutional growth with the searching question: “But what became of the Lord’s congregation?”
* The conclusion calls for a different method of building.
* True builders must themselves be indwelt by God’s Spirit.
* Human preference—“flesh and blood”—must cease to govern.
* God’s good and perfect will alone must determine the work.
* The sermon ends with the imperative: “Let us begin in faith.”

Chairman’s report

* The previous annual meeting established laws and rules for a Lutheran Free Church.
* The present meeting must carry the work forward.
* The principles are claimed to be ancient Christian principles rather than innovations.
* Hauge’s movement is presented as their decisive historical manifestation among Norwegian Lutherans.
* The Haugean awakening generated conflict between awakened laity and high-church or spiritually dead clergy.
* That historical conflict is said to have continued in America.
* The Free Church’s purpose is restated as spiritual awakening and ecclesiastical freedom.
* Opposition from other Norwegian Lutheran church bodies is then presented as evidence of the continuing conflict.
* Quotations and criticisms of the Norwegian Synod and United Church sharpen the contrast between personal conscience and denominational obedience.
* The Free Church’s task is defined positively: freedom of conscience and personal Christian life in congregations.
* The seminary belongs directly to that task.
* Institutional uncertainty over Augsburg property is subordinated to spiritual confidence.
* The argument culminates in the principle that the Free Church’s existence does not depend on earthly property.

Seminary and mission reports

* Institutional difficulties are acknowledged.
* Divine blessing is nevertheless emphasized.
* Growth in students, missionary commitments, finances, and conversions is reported as evidence of spiritual fruit.
* Opposition is anticipated rather than denied.
* Future labor therefore requires faith, love, and perseverance.
* Hope is conditional upon faithfulness: if they are found faithful to the last, they shall see the Lord’s help and rejoice in the fruit of their work.

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

* 1 Corinthians 3:10–17 is explicitly named as the sermon text. It supplies the central imagery of foundation, builders, materials, testing, and God’s temple.
* “Jesus Christ” as the sole foundation directly reflects 1 Corinthians 3:11.
* “chief cornerstone” echoes Ephesians 2:20 and 1 Peter 2:6.
* “living stones” and “a spiritual house, a holy priesthood” come from 1 Peter 2:4–5.
* The source says, “He says in another of his letters,” apparently referring back to Paul, yet the quoted wording corresponds to 1 Peter 2:4–5. This apparent attribution in the source should not be silently corrected in translation.
* “without spot or wrinkle” echoes Ephesians 5:27.
* “light and salt in the world” echoes Matthew 5:13–14.
* “the city on the hill, which cannot be hidden” echoes Matthew 5:14.
* “members of his body” evokes 1 Corinthians 12, Romans 12, and Ephesians 4–5.
* Judas among the disciples evokes the Gospel narratives.
* “flesh and blood” strongly recalls Galatians 1:16.
* “God’s good and perfect will” echoes Romans 12:2.
* “begin in faith” is not a direct quotation but belongs to the pervasive New Testament/Lutheran vocabulary of living by faith.
* “conversion and salvation of souls” reflects the evangelical and pietistic biblical vocabulary of conversion and salvation.
* “fruit of our labor” echoes widespread New Testament fruit imagery.
* “be found faithful to the last” resonates with Revelation 2:10 and New Testament exhortations to perseverance.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

* The sermon’s warning about a bad foundation must retain the image of the house collapsing and the builders themselves being buried beneath the ruins.
* “We all bear our guilt” must remain direct and unqualified.
* The critique of gathering people into congregations without asking about spiritual life must retain its accusatory force.
* “But what became of the Lord’s congregation?” must remain a genuine rhetorical judgment rather than be softened into a question about institutional effectiveness.
* The Free Church’s conflict with other church bodies must remain historically polemical where the source is polemical.
* “pest” in the quotation from Koren should remain forceful because that is the force of the historical quotation.
* The claim that the United Church placed denominational obedience above conviction and conscience must remain categorical.
* The description of Augsburg’s struggle must retain the contrast between those who wanted “Augsburg’s property” but not “Augsburg’s spirit.”

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

* “maa Gud selv faa lede os” → “God himself must be allowed to lead us,” not “may lead us.”
* “maa opbygges” → “must be built up.”
* “enhver se til” → “let each one take heed” or “each one must take heed,” preserving admonitory force.
* “vil have” in “Gud vil have” → “God wills to have” or “God will have,” not merely “God would like.”
* “maa begynde” → “must begin.”
* “maa Guds Aand faa tage Bolig” → “God’s Spirit must be allowed to dwell.”
* “maa ikke raadspørge” → “must not consult.”
* “maa være” in the Synod quotation → “must be.”
* “faar … arbeide” conveys the assigned necessity of the Free Church’s task: “must labor.”
* “maa vi gjøre os fortrolige med” → “we must reconcile ourselves to” / “we must become accustomed to the fact.”
* “vil … møde” → firm expectation: “will meet.”
* “vil Tro, Kjærlighed og Udholdenhed til” → “it will require faith, love, and perseverance.”

STEP 7: CONSISTENT THEOLOGICAL TERM RENDERING

* Menighed → “congregation” when an organized local body is clearly meant; “Church” or “congregation of God” when the theological body of believers is in view.
* Tro → faith.
* retfærdiggjøres → is justified.
* Guds Aand → God’s Spirit.
* aandeligt Liv → spiritual life.
* levende → living.
* levendegjort / Levendegjørelse → made alive / spiritual quickening, depending on grammatical context.
* helliget → sanctified.
* renset → cleansed.
* Omvendelse → conversion.
* Frelse → salvation.
* Samvittighed → conscience.
* Vækkelse → awakening.
* Naade → grace.
* Herrens Menighed → the Lord’s congregation / Church according to theological context.
* Herrens Vidner → witnesses of the Lord.
* Guds Rige → the Kingdom of God.
* Presteskole → theological seminary when referring institutionally to Augsburg’s ministerial training program.

STEP 8: METAPHOR INTEGRITY PRESERVATION

* Foundation remains foundation.
* Cornerstone remains cornerstone.
* Builders remain builders.
* Gold, silver, costly stones, wood, hay, and straw remain literal building materials.
* Living stones remain living stones.
* Temple remains temple.
* Body and members remain bodily imagery.
* Spot and wrinkle remain visible blemish imagery.
* Light, salt, and city remain their biblical forms.
* Spiritual fruit remains fruit.
* God’s Spirit taking up residence remains a dwelling metaphor and should not be reduced to “influencing us.”

STEP 9: TONE CONTRAST PRESERVATION

* The hopeful opening—desire for blessing—is immediately subjected to the severe condition of divine guidance.
* The constructive image of building becomes a warning of collapse.
* The affirmation of Christ as foundation leads to examination of the builders.
* The ideal of a holy Church is followed by acknowledgment of Judas and impurity within apostolic congregations.
* That concession then turns sharply back into rebuke of contemporary laxity.
* Corporate guilt interrupts institutional pride.
* The reports alternate between controversy, financial anxiety, opposition, blessing, numerical growth, missionary zeal, and confidence in God’s help.
* The loss of property is treated seriously but is immediately contrasted with the spiritual independence of the Church from earthly possessions.

STEP 10: ORAL CADENCE AWARENESS

* The sermon should retain its long conditional openings and cumulative reasoning.
* Short pivots should remain short: “No, we all bear our guilt.”
* Repetition must be heard rather than edited away: “large masses, large congregations, large church bodies.”
* The recurring “But,” “Now,” “Thus,” and “If” markers should remain because they guide oral argument.
* The concluding exhortation “Let us begin in faith” should retain its abrupt, declarative finality.
* Administrative minutes should remain comparatively clipped, thereby preserving the contrast between spoken exhortation and recorded proceedings.

STEP 11: ACADEMIC TRANSLATION

The Free Church’s Second Annual Meeting
June 8–12, 1898
——

The Lutheran Free Church assembled for its annual meeting in Trinity Church, Pastor Gjertsen’s charge, Minneapolis, Minnesota, on June 8, 1898, at 10:30 in the morning.

The meeting was opened with the singing of Hymn 433, verses 1–5. Pastor Chr. Nrehus then delivered the opening sermon on 1 Corinthians 3:10–17.

Now that, after the passing of a year, we are again assembled for an annual meeting here in this church, it is surely with the desire and prayer in our hearts that the meeting may become a blessing to us and to our Church. But if this is to happen, then God himself must be allowed to lead us by his Spirit and by his Word. Only then are we enabled to do the Lord’s work in truth.

According to our text, we will speak a little about the Lord’s temple and its building up.

First, the foundation. When one is to build, it is of great importance that a good foundation be laid; for if one lays a poor foundation, one risks the house falling down and oneself being buried beneath its ruins. So also with the congregation of God. If a good foundation is not laid, the whole structure will collapse.

Now there is only one foundation upon which a true congregation of the Lord can be built, namely Jesus Christ. He is the great chief cornerstone upon which we all, as living stones, must be built up, so that together we may constitute a holy temple for the Lord. It was upon this foundation that the Apostle Paul built. It was the same foundation upon which Luther built when, as the foundation of his life’s work, he laid this truth: that the human being is justified by faith in Christ alone.

Christ, then, is the foundation; but let each one take heed how he builds upon it. It is this admonition of the Apostle that we especially wish to lay upon one another’s hearts at this hour. It is an exceedingly important work, this work of taking part in the building of God’s temple, and we do well to examine ourselves, whether we build well and whether we use the right material.

The Apostle names several things as materials—gold, silver, costly stones, wood, hay, straw. He assumes that all these different materials will be used. He does not here specifically commend any one of them; yet it is nevertheless evident from the context and from other statements of the same Apostle that the right material is that which is here called gold, silver, and costly stones. He says in another of his letters: Come to him, the living Stone, indeed rejected by men, but chosen and precious before God, and yourselves, as living stones, be built up into a spiritual house, a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ.

The right material, then, is living stones, stones that have been made alive, cleansed, and sanctified by God’s Spirit.

There is no doubt that God wills to have a living, pure, holy Church, without spot or wrinkle. It is merely an excuse when one speaks of the invisible Church in order to cover over the infirmities of that which we call the congregation among us.

But there is little comfort in this for those who, according to God’s Word, believe that the Church is to be light and salt in the world, the city upon the hill which cannot be hidden. There can be no doubt what the Lord calls his congregation. It is the gathering of all those who through faith in Jesus Christ have become living members of his body, which is the Church.

It is another matter whether here on earth we can bring the Church forth in its true form as a gathering consisting only of believing children of God who have been cleansed and sanctified by the blood of Christ.

We find that this did not succeed even for the Savior himself; for among the little company of disciples there was also a Judas. And the picture that the Apostles’ letters give us of the first congregations also indicates that within them as well there was much that was impure.

But this is nevertheless no reason why we should continue in this country as we have done until now—practically driving our people together into congregations without asking whether there is spiritual life and real Christianity. I do not mention this in order especially to blame particular men or to place the guilt upon any single church body.

No, we all bear our guilt.

Many of us did it in ignorance; we understood no better. In many ways we were zealous in establishing congregations, church bodies, churches, and schools. We gathered people in great masses, large congregations, large church bodies, and we thought that things were going well. But what became of the Lord’s congregation?

We must begin another course of procedure, and let us build from this day onward.

If we are to become true builders, God’s Spirit must be allowed to take up his dwelling in our hearts, and we must not consult flesh and blood as to what I want, or what anyone else wants, but only look to what God’s good and perfect will is. Let us begin in faith.

Wednesday Afternoon

Devotion by Pastor O. Dahle of Aitkin, Minnesota. The times for the business sessions were fixed at 9:30–12:00 in the morning and 2:00–5:00 in the afternoon.

It was resolved that a prayer meeting be held each day from 9:00 to 9:30 in the morning, and that the chairman appoint a man to lead these prayer meetings. It was proposed and resolved that the evening services begin at 8:00.

The chairman was instructed to appoint tellers. It was resolved that the chairman likewise appoint a credentials committee of three members.

The following were appointed tellers: Pastor C. Berntsen, Pastor J. M. Jakobsen, Pastor H. N. Hendrickson, and Pastor J. C. Fossum.

The chairman stated that all voting members of Lutheran congregations, regardless of denominational affiliation, could become voting members of this meeting by signing the following:

“The undersigned hereby declares that he approves the Rules of the Free Church and will work for its purpose as stated in § 2.”

The following were appointed to the credentials committee: Pastor A. J. Løgeland, Prof. M. Pettersen, and Mr. H. Paulson.

It was resolved to hear the reports.

The chairman, Pastor E. P. Harbo of Duluth, first read his report:

At last year’s annual meeting, the Free Church work advanced so far that laws and rules for a Lutheran Free Church were considered and in part adopted. It now falls to this annual meeting to continue the work further. The principles that underlie the work of the Free Church are not new. They are as old as Christianity itself. But among our people it was not until Hans Nielsen Hauge that they began in earnest to make themselves felt. With the Haugean Awakening in Norway there arose a division within the Church, a struggle between the awakened laity and the high-church and, in part, spiritually dead pastors within the State Church.

This struggle has continued here in this country. Those who favor ecclesiastical freedom have here set themselves the goal “to work for the quickening and liberation of all Norwegian Lutheran congregations, so that, according to their calling and ability, they may labor in spiritual independence and freedom for the cause of God’s Kingdom at home and abroad.” This was also established last year as the purpose of the Free Church, and we might readily have expected that all Norwegian Lutheran church bodies in this country would be willing to assent to such a purpose. Nevertheless, we see that they rise up in opposition to it.

The position of the Norwegian Synod in this regard was expressed by Chairman Koren at the Synod’s annual meeting in 1896:

“There is a phenomenon that is well known, though perhaps not so well understood, both in Norway and here in America, and which goes under the name of the western Norwegian Awakening, the sorrowful fruits of which, of various kinds, will remain fresh in the memory and can be recognized year after year by those who have a reasonably exercised discernment and insight into God’s Word. Unfortunately, it has extended much farther than many suppose. From it we will pray God graciously to preserve us. But if we are to have anything capable of standing against this pest, then it must be a true, sound Christian life.”

The United Church consists chiefly of Anti-Missourians who in spirit and disposition are still good Synod people. In this way it can also be explained why the United Church has with such bitterness persecuted those who have labored for awakening and spiritual life within the congregations.

In the United Church it is no longer a question of obedience to conviction and conscience, but first and foremost of obedience to the church body.

The task of the Free Church in this country is therefore plain and clear.

Amid tribulation and opposition, it must labor for freedom of conscience and personal Christian life within the congregations. To this task belongs also the maintenance of a theological seminary whose first aim is to train earnest, living witnesses of the Lord.

Augsburg Seminary has been such a school. In spite of the difficulties, the Seminary has also during the past year been highly blessed by the Lord. Enrollment has been greater than ever before. Eighteen young men graduated from the college department and nine from the theological department. The school, however, has not received the financial support that was needed, which may possibly be due to the unfortunate lawsuit concerning the property and the state of uncertainty into which the school has thereby been brought.

Whatever the outcome of the case may be, it will not have great influence upon the future of the Free Church. Its continued existence does not depend upon earthly property.

The possible loss of the property may, however, make it necessary for this annual meeting to take up for consideration the question of a future home for the theological seminary.

Since the previous annual meeting, Candidate J. Eliasen and Student C. Østerhus have been ordained.

During the past year as well, the work of edification throughout the congregations has been carried on with vigor and has borne good fruit in the conversion and salvation of souls.

Pastor P. Nilsen has again this year traveled about among our people and labored to great blessing. His work should be continued during the coming year as well.

Prof. Sverdrup read the reports of the mission committees, since the mission secretary, Prof. J. H. Blegen, was absent on a journey to Norway. The Committee for Mission to the Heathen had held four meetings during the year. Missionary concern appeared to be increasing strongly, as could be seen, among other things, from the fact that by the close of the year support had been pledged for twenty native teachers.

The committee had resolved to call and send out Candidate J. B. Jerstad as a missionary; likewise, it had already sent out two deaconesses to take charge of the orphanage in place of the late Sister Pernille Pedersen. The sisters at the Deaconess Home had collected about $300 toward their journey.

Dr. J. Dyrnes and Candidate N. Hatlem had both offered themselves for service as missionaries. Missionary Tou, who is presently at home on a visit, is to travel among the congregations and speak on behalf of the mission cause. During the year, $5,437.58 had been received into the foreign mission treasury. Approximately $7,000.00 would be needed for the next year.

The Home Mission Committee had held seven meetings. Its treasury had received $2,559.95.

At least $3,000 would be needed for the next year. The committee wished especially to direct the annual meeting’s attention to the missionary need around Fosston, Hawley, and Wahpeton.

The treasurer of the teachers’ salary fund, Prof. J. L. Nydahl, reported that from August 31, 1897, to June 1, 1898, $3,579.24 had been received for the theological seminary through voluntary contributions.

The Board of Trustees was not yet ready to report; the chairman of the Board, Prof. S. Oftedal, did, however, read the report of the Seminary’s director.

From this it appeared that the theological seminary had this year been blessed by the Lord in a special degree, in spite of the painful position into which the lawsuit over the property had brought it. This unfortunate lawsuit has, however, clearly shown that it is Augsburg’s property, but not Augsburg’s spirit, that the United Church wants.

Even if Augsburg’s property is lost, the work cannot therefore cease. But we must reconcile ourselves to the fact that Augsburg’s work for living pastors and living congregations will meet fierce opposition also in the future, and that it will require faith, love, and perseverance. But then we shall also be permitted to behold the Lord’s help and rejoice in the fruit of our labor, provided that we are found faithful to the last.

The teachers at the school had this year been the same as before; but greater teaching resources and more room were needed. Instruction in English also had to be continued in the theological department.

A class in French had been established this year and led by the director.

The number of students this year had been 194, compared with 187 the year before. Eighteen young men graduated from the college department and nine from the theological department. Of the latter, two had a particular call to become missionaries.

A report from the Free Church Bookstore was read. The business had made good progress. Most of the surplus had been added to the capital fund. An appropriation of $200.00 had been made to Augsburg Seminary.

The chairman read letters to the annual meeting from Pastors M. Falk Gjertsen and E. Gynild, as well as Prof. Reimestad, all of whom were traveling in Norway. A letter from Pastor O. J. Edwards, Cooperstown, North Dakota, was also read.

It was resolved to proceed to the election of officers. Pastor Harbo was unanimously reelected chairman.

The following were nominated for secretary: Pastor H. Yderstad, Pastor A. Helland, and Pastor H. Urseth. Yderstad received 16 votes, Helland 91, and Urseth 22.

Helland was declared elected. He stated that he could not well accept the election. It was then determined to elect an assistant secretary, and Pastor Yderstad was elected assistant secretary. Yderstad resigned, whereupon the question arose: How, then, are the railroad certificates to be endorsed? After some discussion concerning this, Pastor Yderstad was instructed to investigate the matter. Meanwhile, Pastor Helland asked leave to accept the election as secretary. The resignation was not accepted.

The following were nominated as members of the nominating committee: Prof. J. L. Nydahl, Pastor D. Paulson, Pastor E. Aas, Prof. G. Sverdrup, Mr. Jonas Hetland, Mr. Baard Andersen, and Fingar Fosliet.

Nydahl was elected but resigned. Paulson, Aas, Sverdrup, Hetland, and Andersen were elected. Sverdrup resigned, and Fingar Fosliet was elected in his place.

On the motion of Prof. Sverdrup, it was resolved to take up the “Rules for the Free Church” for consideration on Thursday morning.

In the evening, Pastors A. Halvorson and M. B. Sveen preached in Trinity Church, and Pastors E. Aas and N. J. Thomasberg in St. Luke’s Church.
